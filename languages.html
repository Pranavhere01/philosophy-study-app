1. The decision that shapes everything

"German-taught" is a language gate, not a geography gate. To enrol in a degree taught in German you must already hold a certified B2–C1 in German on the day you apply — not on the day you arrive. The universities are the easy part; the certificate is the project.

This page therefore covers three things together, because they are one decision:

  • German — the German-medium (German-taught) degree programmes, grouped by country, with the cost and the language proof each one expects.
  • English — the fallback that keeps the breadth: the same universities offer English-taught degrees you can enter now, while you learn German on the side.
  • Sanskrit — a short background, because the philosophy source texts behind this study path are written in it, and reading the terms precisely beats memorising English glosses.

The honest rule

Reach C1 before the application deadline, not during. German bachelor's programmes are overwhelmingly German-taught; master's programmes are increasingly English-taught. If the goal is to study soon rather than in German, start on the English track (§7) and keep German running in parallel.

7. English — the fallback that keeps the breadth

English is the working language of global academia, and it is the reason you do not have to wait for C1 German to move. The same German, Austrian and Swiss universities offer thousands of English-taught degrees — especially master's — that you can enter with an English test (IELTS/TOEFL) instead of a German certificate.

The trade-off, stated plainly

English-taught = start now; the language gate is IELTS/TOEFL; the degree is the same, and in Germany it is usually still tuition-free under the same rules. German-taught = the full immersion and the widest programme choice, but only after you hold C1. Most people sequence them: learn German on the side while applying to English-taught master's, then study in German for a later degree or stay in the country on the strength of the language.

  • Search the same DAAD database with "language of instruction = English".
  • The study app already carries a funded-PhD & no-tuition master's catalogue in English — the guide and the 65-programme map across 48 countries — which is the English-language breadth you should read alongside this page.
  • Note the fee difference outside DACH: English-taught master's in the Netherlands, the Nordics and the UK are not free for non-EU students, whereas German-taught and many German English-taught public programmes are.

8. Sanskrit — a short background (as a language)

Sanskrit (saṃskṛtam, संस्कृतम् — "perfected, refined") is an Old Indo-Aryan language of the Indo-European family, and the language in which the Indian philosophical canon was written — the Vedas and Upaniṣads, and the classical systems (Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) alongside Buddhist and Jaina Sanskrit literature.

Three facts that make it load-bearing here

  • Grammar was codified extremely early and precisely. Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 4th century BCE) is a generative rule-system of ~4,000 rules that remains the reference grammar — arguably the first formal grammar of any language.
  • It is highly inflected and euphonic. Eight cases, three numbers, three genders, and sandhi (systematic sound combination across word boundaries) — which is why a single term has many surface forms and why transliteration must be exact.
  • Scholarly citation uses IAST — the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration with full diacritics (ā ī ū ṛ ṝ ḷ ṣ ś ṃ ḥ ṅ ñ). This is the convention the study module's glossary follows, so anumāna, pramāṇa, vyāpti and pratītyasamutpāda are spelled with their marks, not flattened to ASCII.

Why it matters to a philosophy student

UGC NET Philosophy Paper II Unit 1 (Vedic and Upaniṣadic thought, Cārvāka, Jainism, Buddhism, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) and IGNOU MPY-001 are built on Sanskrit technical vocabulary — pramāṇa (means of knowledge), anumāna (inference), vyāpti (invariable concomitance), hetvābhāsa (fallacy), ātman, brahman, mokṣa, duḥkha, śabda. Reading the term precisely — and knowing what its root and compounds contribute — beats memorising a one-line English gloss, and it is the single cheapest way to stop confusing near-synonyms in the exam.

Minimal resources (reputable, mostly free)

  • Samskrita Bharati — spoken/conversational Sanskrit.
  • Central Sanskrit University — government university and programmes.
  • NPTEL — free video courses; search "Sanskrit".
  • Cologne Digital Sanskrit Dictionaries — Monier-Williams, Apte and others online.
  • learnsanskrit.cc — quick dictionary and transliteration lookup.

11. Caveats & sources

  • Curated, not exhaustive. Germany alone has ~400 higher-education institutions; this page lists the major ones and points to the DAAD database for the rest.
  • Tuition figures are as of 2025 and change (TUM's fees are new; other states are debating them). Re-verify on the university's own page.
  • German-taught ≠ automatically free. Private universities (and now TUM) charge even in Germany.
  • The language certificate is the real gate. Plan the timeline around reaching C1, not around the admission season.
  • For an Indian applicant: check credential recognition (Anabin/uni-assist) and the DAAD India scholarship pages, which fund German-language study and the Studienkolleg route.
